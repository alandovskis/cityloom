# Market Trends — Streetmix at City Scale

## Why This Matters Here

The upstream `intent-statement.md` records the initiative's trigger as a gap in
existing tools rather than an external deadline. That means nothing outside the
project is forcing a date. This report asks a different question: are there
currents that make the gap worth filling now rather than at any other time?

Three were selected as relevant: open data and basemaps, policy and funding
shifts toward street redesign, and rising expectation of public participation
(Q4).

Each is treated below as a **hypothesis with supporting evidence**, not as an
established fact, in line with the evidence standard for this phase.

## Trend 1 — Open Data and Basemaps

**Hypothesis:** street network data is now broadly available enough that a
map-grounded design tool can work in many cities without per-city data deals.

**Supporting evidence.** A/B Street states that it works anywhere in the world
thanks to OpenStreetMap [1]. That is an existence proof rather than a market
statistic: a network-scale street tool has already been built on open data and
run against arbitrary cities.

**What it does not establish.** Nothing found quantifies the coverage or quality
of that data across cities, or whether it is detailed enough for street-level
design (curb lines, lane widths, parking) as opposed to network topology. That
distinction matters directly for this initiative and is unresolved. [assumption]

## Trend 2 — Policy and Funding Shifts Toward Street Redesign

**Hypothesis:** public money and policy attention are moving toward street
redesign, which increases the number of street redesign conversations a tool
could serve.

**Supporting evidence.** In the United States, the federal Safe Streets and Roads
for All programme has continued to make funding available to local, regional and
Tribal communities, with planning and demonstration grants described as more
accessible than the implementation grants [2]. Concrete redesign outcomes are
being reported against that funding — Columbus used the programme on a
high-injury street and reported reduced crashes between 2024 and 2025, and Fort
Lauderdale's NE 15th Avenue reconfiguration reported the share of drivers
exceeding the 35 mph limit falling from 60% to 1%, sustained a year later [2].
Cities are planning multi-year: Minneapolis's Vision Zero Action Plan contains
more than 80 actions planned for 2026 through 2030 [3].

**The counter-signal, recorded deliberately.** The same sources note that some
cities' bond funds will be substantially spent by 2026, and that federal safety
grant funding faces an uncertain future [2]. This trend is therefore **not
uniformly favourable** — the volume of activity is real, but its funding base is
not secure. Presenting it as a straightforward tailwind would overstate it.

**Geographic limit.** All the evidence found for this trend is from the United
States. Whether it holds elsewhere was not established, and the addressable
audience has not been geographically bounded (Q6 selected an audience of
advocates and the public rather than a region). [assumption]

## Trend 3 — Rising Expectation of Public Participation

**Hypothesis:** the expectation that the public gets a substantive say in
infrastructure decisions is rising, which grows the audience for a tool that lets
a non-professional produce a credible proposal.

**Supporting evidence.** A market of participation platforms exists and is
investing: Konveio turns static plans into interactive commentable documents and
reports an AI-assisted pilot with UK councils achieving an 85% reduction in staff
time for summarising citizen feedback [4]; Social Pinpoint offers more than 40
engagement tools spanning surveys, forums, mapping and budgeting [5]. Streetmix's
own stated purpose is to create a web version of the paper cut-out street design
activity used in public meetings, promoting two-way communication between
planners and the public [6].

**How to read it.** The evidence establishes that a participation *industry*
exists and is being bought, which is a stronger signal than survey sentiment. It
does not establish a rate of change, so "rising" remains the hypothesis rather
than the finding. [assumption]

**Why it is the most relevant of the three.** These platforms serve the
consultation step — collecting reactions to a plan someone else drew. The
addressable audience selected for this initiative is advocates and the public
(Q6), and the differentiator selected is output a city will accept in a formal
process (Q3). That positions this initiative one step upstream of where the
participation market currently sits: not commenting on a proposal, but making
one.

## Regulatory Shifts

No regulatory shift was identified as relevant. Street design standards are
jurisdictional, and no jurisdiction has been established for this initiative —
`intent-statement.md` records the absence of any geographic, jurisdictional, or
street-design-standards context as an open assumption. Until that is settled,
regulatory analysis has nothing to attach to. [assumption]

## Net Reading

Two of the three trends are supported by concrete evidence (funding activity,
participation-platform investment) and one is supported by an existence proof
(open data). None is strong enough on its own to constitute a market timing
argument, and one carries an explicit counter-signal about funding durability.

Taken together they support a modest claim: **the conditions for this tool are
present, and they are not obviously improving or deteriorating.** They do not
support a claim that a window is opening or closing. The initiative's own
justification remains the one recorded in `intent-statement.md` — a gap in the
tools — rather than external timing. [assumption]

## Assumptions & Open Questions

- Whether open street data is detailed enough for street-level design rather than
  network topology is unresolved and is the single most consequential unknown in
  this report. [assumption]
- All funding and policy evidence is United States-specific; applicability
  elsewhere is unestablished. [assumption]
- "Rising" participation expectation is inferred from the existence and
  investment of a platform market, not from a measured trend line. [assumption]
- No regulatory analysis was possible without a jurisdiction. [assumption]
- Trend evidence was gathered at a point in time (September 2026) and is not
  monitored; nothing in this workflow refreshes it. [assumption]
